## What Really Happened

Ever since the war in Iran began on February 28, 2026, the Philippines, which imports about 98% of its oil from the Gulf, saw local diesel and gasoline prices more than double. On March 24, President Marcos declared a state of national energy emergency, with the order set to remain in effect for one year. With the Energy Secretary reporting that the country only had around 45 days of fuel supply, the Philippines became the first ever nation to make a declaration of this kind.

Many people assumed the Philippines would be shielded because it buys mostly finished products, like gasoline and diesel, from its Asian neighbors. But the Energy Secretary explained that this was a domino effect: refiners in China, South Korea, Singapore and Japan all get their crude oil from the Middle East, meaning the price rises still have a direct impact. Another common assumption that is simply inaccurate is that the crisis faded once the headlines did. However, the war continues to disrupt oil production and shipping, and while the Department of Energy (DOE) says October’s fuel supply is secured, it is still monitoring whether November and December will need another shipment.

## Current Situation and How This Affects People’s Everyday Lives

For most households, the price shock is still very much felt. Inflation hit 7.2% in September, the fastest pace in over three years, while gasoline and diesel prices saw a 40.9% and 61.3% increase in a year, with electricity prices rising 14.6%. Additionally, low-income households faced an even sharper 9% inflation rate, highlighting the significance on the lower side of the social ladder. Officials said the September jump was driven mainly by weather-related food supply disruptions and higher global oil prices, and rice, the staple, was up 20.3%.

Those costs are especially visible on the road. Fare hikes approved in March were suspended for months, but on September 28 the minimum jeepney fare rose from ₱13 to ₱14, and modern jeepneys now charge at least ₱17 instead of ₱15. For many drivers, even a subtle increase like this makes it much more difficult to keep up, even with help: select drivers and operators receive a fuel subsidy of ₱12 per liter, about $0.19. The strain was on display when transport strikes began on September 28 and some jeepney drivers and their families publicly shared meager meals of dried fish and tomatoes.

Meanwhile, in the Visayas, people are dealing with the dark as well as the price of fuel. In Iloilo City, rotational outages affect at least 20,000 households a day, with about 40 megawatts shed daily, roughly a third of the city’s demand. In Cebu, brownouts have grown longer, though feeders serving hospitals are excluded as a basic protocol, and electricity there has reached a record ₱14.96 per kWh, about $0.24. Small businesses feel it most. Cebu’s governor said every brownout means lost income for small businesses, and business groups report that these firms are absorbing the cost of generators, fuel and equipment repairs. While large companies have the ability to cope, market stalls and small shops often cannot.

None of this arrived in isolation. In May, a 4,000-megawatt power loss triggered rolling blackouts across Luzon and the Visayas, well before the fare hikes and the September food price surge. Households have had to absorb one shock after another without time to recover from the last.

## Why You Need to Care Now

The Philippines has little cushion left. Its roughly 45 days of diesel supply fell well short of the 90 days of import cover the International Energy Agency recommends, and relief for the grid is not guaranteed: officials say returning plants could improve supply from mid-November through December, but they cannot promise brownouts will end, and Visayas regional councils are asking the president to declare a power emergency. Calls for the Energy Secretary’s resignation have returned as well. With the war unresolved and the year’s final months of fuel not yet fully lined up, the pressure on Filipino families is not fading but moving to new places.
