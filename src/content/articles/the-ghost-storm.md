## What Really Happened

On the morning of September 1, 2026, Tropical Depression Pilandok (international name Krovanh) intensified into a tropical storm about 1015 kilometers east of extreme Northern Luzon. But, in contrast to the commonly held misunderstanding, this storm never even got close to the Philippines. In fact, the Philippine Atmospheric, Geophysical and Astronomical Services Administration (PAGASA), which is the official national weather service agency of the Philippines, never raised a single wind signal anywhere in the country because of it.

So, what actually happened and how does a storm that stays that far away end up having such a significant impact on the Philippines?

Following its intensification, the storm drifted northwestward over the Philippine Sea at a modest 10 kph, staying consistently distant from Philippine soil. In fact, by 4:30 a.m. on Thursday, September 3, Pilandok had left the Philippine Area of Responsibility (PAR) entirely. Once outside PAR, it actually strengthened a bit, with maximum sustained winds climbing to 85 km/h and gustiness up to 105 km/h, as it headed toward southern Japan.

The real story is the southwest monsoon. During its build-up, Pilandok's circulation had pulled moisture into and intensified the habagat (southwest monsoon) that was already active over the Philippines. This would be backed by PAGASA's own statements, describing Pilandok as "enhancing the southwest monsoon," rather than having a direct impact on the nation itself. Ranging from 50 to 100 millimeters, the southwest monsoon would bring moderate to heavy rain to at least 9 provinces by September 3.

## Current Situation and How This Affects People's Everyday Lives

While the tropical storm Pilandok and the southwest monsoon did not directly cause any structural damages or raise any tropical cyclone wind signals, its impact is significant on a nationwide scale. This is primarily because of the critical timing of the storm, which arrived immediately after a string of other tropical cyclones, including Luis, Maymay, and Neneng. What this means is that the southwest monsoon dumped massive rainfall onto soil and infrastructure that were already completely saturated from the previous storms, further enhancing the damage that was already done.

Notably, the recent storms have contributed to the ₱12.4 billion (\~$198 million) worth of agricultural loss. The heavy flooding drowned more than 31,000 hectares of farmland, affecting over 23,000 farmers across regions like the Cordillera Administrative Region (CAR), Ilocos, and Cagayan Valley. Furthermore, nationwide infrastructure damage reached ₱866 million (\~$13.8 million), with the Department of Health reporting over ₱54 million (\~$860,000) in damages specifically to local health facilities.

The cumulative impact from the recent damages caused by the storms holds a significant impact on the daily survival and livelihoods of ordinary Filipinos. Farming families, many of whom rely on a single harvest cycle for their household income, have been pushed into deeper debt as flooded fields wipe out crops they had already invested months of labor and borrowed capital into. Displacement, another major outcome of the storm, compounds this vulnerability: with thousands of houses damaged or destroyed across the affected regions, many families, including young children, have been forced into evacuation centers, often for extended stretches, where access to clean water, nutrition, and basic sanitation is not guaranteed at all times.

For children specifically, this kind of prolonged disruption carries risks that go beyond the immediate discomfort of displacement. Flooded or damaged school buildings often result in an increased number of classroom days missed, which stacks up to an already strained academic year. Moreover, the stress and instability of repeated evacuations can affect a child's sense of safety and routine in ways that are neither visible nor quantifiable. Meanwhile, the ₱54 million in damage to local health facilities results in reduced capacity to treat waterborne illness, respiratory infections, and injuries that tend to spike in flood-affected communities. This tends to leave children and the elderly, who are typically the most medically vulnerable, with fewer options to receive care when needed most.

## Beyond the Storm

The cumulative damage done by Pilandok and the previous storms that came weeks before is not a new occurrence in the Philippines. This is the pattern that repeats across the country nearly every storm season: continuous waves of storms across the span of multiple weeks that keep coming before the people have the time and ability to recover.

Supporting these communities, whether through direct aid, agricultural recovery programs, or investment in more resilient infrastructure and health facilities, is necessary as a response to a recurring reality that the Philippines faces year after year. Otherwise, it will always continue to be the most vulnerable citizens, farmers, children, and displaced families that carry the heaviest share of the cost.
