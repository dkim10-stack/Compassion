## What Really Happened

On September 28, 2026, an airstrike hit the town of Kyauktaw in Myanmar’s Rakhine State. The Arakan Army (AA), the ethnic armed group that controls the area, claimed that 50 people were killed and at least 58 were wounded, with a local aid worker later confirming the numbers. It was the deadliest strike in Rakhine since a hospital bombing that took place in December 2025. But in contrast to the widely depicted front-line battles between the military and rebel armies, it is the ignored detail of what the attack had hit that highlights the greater impact this attack has. The market and the bridge beside it had become a busy trading hub during the war, thriving on a river route to India that had become a lifeline.

A large majority of conflict in the front lines at Rakhine has paused recently. By 2025, the AA held effective control of most of the state, apart from the towns of Sittwe, Kyaukphyu and Manaung, after sweeping gains between late 2023 and late 2024. Since then, the fighting can be characterized as being much more siege-like, with blockades, artillery exchanges and the military’s heavy use of airpower shifting to be the primary method of engaging in conflict. The fighting has certainly not stopped. This month, battles erupted around Nga Pyi Island in the Kaladan River delta, which the AA is using to besiege the state capital, Sittwe, with the military reportedly having sent gunboats in an attempt to capture the island.

## Why Sittwe and Kyaukphyu Matter

Sittwe, Rakhine’s capital and its main port, relies almost entirely on boats from Yangon for its food supply, and tens of thousands of residents are trapped by fighting in the surrounding areas. Kyaukphyu, further south, matters for a different reason. Beijing has pledged an estimated $9 billion for a deep-water port there. The port has been largely stalled by the fighting, but the town also has a completed China-backed power plant and gas pipeline, and a naval base. The AA’s chief has declared that all military-held towns in the state will be captured before 2027.

The two towns’ significance lies in the amount of power and leverage it gives to whoever has control over it. Specifically, whoever controls the waterways decides how much rice, fuel and medicine reaches civilians on every side. This is not a new problem. Since November 2023, the military has been blockading main trade routes in Rakhine, holding back food and medicine and driving prices up.

## Current Situation and How This Affects People’s Everyday Lives

As of the end of June 2026, more than 423,700 people were displaced across Rakhine. Aid workers say about 50,000 of them are in Kyaukphyu Township alone, having fled between February and August. Everyday life has been squeezed from every direction.

**Prices:** Those living within Sittwe said they had no option but to travel outside the city by air, but even then air fares were unaffordable. According to market monitoring by the World Food Programme (WFP), prices within the national food basket rose by 20% from February to June 2025, and this increase was particularly severe in Rakhine due to the disruptions in transport and lower quantities of available goods.

**Displaced families and the Rohingya:** Aid cannot reliably reach the people who need it most. Reuters reported last year that restrictions by both the military and the AA left the UN unable to move food beyond Sittwe into central and northern Rakhine. In August 2025, WFP said the share of families in central Rakhine unable to meet basic food needs had risen from 33 percent to 57 percent in eight months, and that northern Rakhine was likely worse. A Rohingya news outlet reported that about 140,000 Rohingya in Sittwe’s camps had gone nearly a year without food distributions. Both the military and the AA have faced accusations of abuses against civilians, including Rohingya.

**Children:** Aid workers told Reuters that more than 100,000 children in Rakhine had acute malnutrition, and fewer than 2 percent could get treatment. Nationwide, 4.9 million children need humanitarian assistance, and 113 of the 487 civilians hurt by landmines and unexploded ordnance in the first half of 2026 were children.

## Why You Need to Care Now

The AA’s chief has indicated that by the end of this year – or perhaps earlier, depending on how things develop – every military-held town in Rakhine will come under attack again. And we know from reports of events happening right now around the entrance to Sittwe that such attacks may soon reach the scale of full-blown battles.

If so then civilians who struggle to find enough rice and fuel to get through another day in their own homes would simply have no place left to run if towns become battlefields. Yet with demand rising and resources falling, agencies such as the WFP (World Food Programme) admit they’re struggling to deliver aid where it’s most needed. It reckons there are 12.4 million people in Myanmar who suffer from acute hunger but can only offer assistance to just 1.5 million of these people.

Falling crises tend to fade from memory even before those responsible take action. But in places like Rakhine, where ordinary citizens are being trampled over by powerful forces both local and foreign, people don’t really have an option of letting this situation pass unnoticed.
